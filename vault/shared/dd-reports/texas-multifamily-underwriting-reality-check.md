# Texas Multifamily Underwriting: The Reality Check
## Most Deals Are Underwritten Wrong. Here's Why That Costs You Millions.

### The Problem Nobody Talks About

Every multifamily deal in Texas starts the same way: someone finds a property, runs some numbers in Excel, and hopes they got it right. The average underwriting session takes 2-4 hours of manual spreadsheet work — rent comps adjusted for vacancy, operating expenses benchmarked against market averages, debt service calculated at assumed financing terms, sensitivity analysis on three scenarios if you're feeling ambitious.

Then the deal gets presented to investors or lenders with a single outcome: GO or NO-GO based on six metrics (IRR, cash-on-cash return, DSCR, LTV, equity multiple, spread). If one of those six numbers misses the threshold, the deal dies regardless of how good the others look.

**Here's what nobody tells you:** Most deals that get rejected under manual underwriting would pass automated analysis — because human spreadsheets are error-prone, inconsistent, and slow enough that you can only run 2-3 scenarios before your competition has already made an offer on a different property.

Meanwhile, the deals that do close often have hidden assumptions baked into those 40-cell spreadsheets: optimistic rent growth rates, understated vacancy percentages, deferred maintenance costs buried in footnotes. These aren't mistakes — they're human limitations at scale.

---

### What Underwriting Actually Should Look Like

A proper underwriting process runs the same deal through:
- **Base case** with conservative market assumptions
- **Downside stress test** (10% rent reduction, 5% expense increase)
- **Refinancing risk analysis** at Year 5 exit
- **Sensitivity tables** on cap rate movement and interest rate scenarios

All of this should produce consistent, auditable outputs — the same methodology applied to every deal, with zero human variability in the calculations.

The problem is that doing this properly takes a trained analyst working for hours. Most investors don't have one. They use spreadsheets written by whoever happened to build them first, and they trust those numbers because they look professional enough.

**This gap between what underwriting should be and what it actually is in practice is worth millions per year across the Texas multifamily market alone.**
