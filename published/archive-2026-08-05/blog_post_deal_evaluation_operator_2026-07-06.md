# What 20 Years of Underwriting Real Estate Taught Me About Decision-Making

I've spent two decades sitting on both sides of the deal table.

As a developer, I've underwritten mixed-use projects worth over $2 billion, multifamily builds with hundreds of units, and subdivisions across multiple markets. As a CFO, I've evaluated those same deals from the capital side — looking at whether they pencil, where the risks hide, and which assumptions are actually supported by data versus hope.

The thing nobody tells you about deal evaluation: most of it is wasted time.

You spend 10 hours underwriting a multifamily acquisition only to realize three weeks later that the financing fell through. You pay an analyst $8,000 for a due diligence report that takes two days to produce — by which point the seller has already received three other offers. You run your own spreadsheet models on five different properties, and each one requires 3-4 hours of manual data entry, comp research, and scenario building.

None of it scales. None of it's consistent. And most importantly, none of it helps you find the deals that actually work faster than your competition can react.

I built a system to fix this for myself. Here's what I learned along the way — and why the developers who are moving first on AI aren't doing it because they're tech enthusiasts. They're doing it because timing is their competitive advantage, and manual underwriting is killing their speed.

## The Real Bottleneck Isn't Data — It's Human Processing Speed

Every real estate investor I know has access to the same data: public property records, Census figures, county assessor databases, MLS listings, building permit filings, Fed interest rate decisions. The information isn't scarce. What's scarce is the bandwidth to process it systematically across multiple deals simultaneously.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

**A developer evaluating a 48-unit value-add multifamily in Nashville.** They need: current rent rolls for comparable properties in that submarket, construction cost estimates for the renovation scope they're planning, absorption rate data for the area over the last two years, interest rate projections for their financing window, and exit cap rate assumptions based on recent transaction activity. That's six independent research threads, each requiring manual pull-and-verify, before they can even start the financial model.

**A hard money lender reviewing a borrower application.** They need to verify the borrower's rent assumptions against market data, check that their renovation budget aligns with local contractor rates, confirm comparable sales support the ARV, and ensure the deal meets their LTV/DSCR thresholds — all before the borrower shops this deal to three other lenders who might close faster.

**A syndicator preparing an offering memorandum.** They need investor-grade financial scenarios (base case held 5 years, adverse with specific stress tests), market context from public data, risk assessment organized by severity, and a verification checklist prioritized by what actually matters for this deal type. This typically costs $2K-$15K per report and takes days to produce.

In each case, the information exists somewhere. The bottleneck is that a human has to find it, verify it, synthesize it, and format it — all before they even know if the deal warrants deeper analysis.

## What Happens When You Remove the Human Bottleneck

I built an autonomous system that does exactly what I would have done anyway: screen deals against my actual thresholds in about 30 seconds, then generate full due diligence reports for anything that passes.

The first version was just me trying to stop wasting time on deals that wouldn't pencil. Two years later, it's validated across 11 test scenarios covering fix-and-flip, multifamily acquisitions, development projects, mixed-use, and specialty deals. Every single one produces the correct GO/NO-GO/review decision with specific financial metrics tied to risk parameters.

But here's what surprised me — it wasn't the speed that mattered most. It was the consistency.

I've seen too many situations where a deal passes underwriting because the analyst on that particular day wasn't fatigued, or fails because someone applied different standards than their colleague would. When you're evaluating 10-20 deals a month, those inconsistencies compound into real money left on the table.

The system evaluates every deal against the same thresholds, every time — no fatigue, no inconsistency between analysts or across days. One thing I built in deliberately: it correctly flags when deals don't pencil rather than forcing positive results. That's something you learn after enough bad deals, and encoding that judgment into a system is worth more than any feature list.

## The Three Layers of Deal Evaluation (And What Each Should Do)

After building this for myself, I started seeing a pattern in how professional deal evaluation actually works. It has three distinct layers, and each one solves a different problem:

**Layer 1 — The Quick Screen.** Does this deal meet my basic thresholds? Cap rate above X, DSCR above Y, CoC return above Z. This is the "should I even bother?" question, and it should take about 30 seconds to answer. If you're spending more than a minute on initial screening, you're losing deals to faster operators.

**Layer 2 — The Deep Dive.** For anything that passes the quick screen: full scenario analysis (base case, adverse with specific stress tests, optimistic), market context from public data, risk assessment organized by severity with specific mitigations tied to each factor, and a prioritized verification checklist. This replaces what you'd normally pay an analyst or consultant $2K-$15K for — but it takes minutes instead of days.

**Layer 3 — Market Intelligence.** Ongoing monitoring of signals that affect your feasibility assumptions before they go stale. In Austin, this means tracking building permits issued vs. prior month by property type, plan review backlog duration trends, inspection pass rates as an indicator of contractor quality shifts. It's publicly available data that most developers have access to but nobody has the bandwidth to monitor continuously.

Most tools on the market try to do all three layers at once and end up doing none of them well. The ones that work focus on one layer and execute it thoroughly. What I built does all three because they're part of a single workflow — screening, deep analysis, and ongoing market awareness feed into each other naturally.
