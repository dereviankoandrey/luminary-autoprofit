## The Problem With "Gut Feel" Investing

Every year, thousands of first-time flippers lose money on deals that looked good at first glance. They fall in love with a property, skip the math, and rationalize through bad numbers. It happens to experienced investors too — especially when they're competing against cash buyers who've already run the analysis.

The difference between profitable flips and expensive mistakes isn't luck. It's **a deterministic evaluation framework** that produces consistent GO / REVIEW / NO-GO decisions you can explain to partners, lenders, or your own conscience at 2 AM.

Here's the system I've used across $4 billion+ in real estate development over two decades.

## Step 1: The Repair Estimate (Where Most Flippers Get Burned)

The biggest single mistake flippers make is underestimating repairs by 20–40%. Here's why:

- **Cosmetic vs structural:** A fresh coat of paint hides foundation cracks, outdated electrical, and plumbing that needs replacement
- **Supply chain reality:** Lumber, fixtures, and labor costs have shifted dramatically since 2020
- **Permit requirements:** What looks like a cosmetic update may require permits, inspections, and plan reviews

**The fix:** Create a line-item repair estimate for every trade. Don't use "per square foot" shortcuts. Every room gets its own budget:

| Trade | Cost Drivers | Typical Range (per unit) |
|-------|-------------|-------------------------|
| Flooring | Demo + disposal, subfloor repair, materials, install | $8–$15/sqft |
| Kitchen cabinets | Demolition, new cabinets, countertops, hardware | $8K–$25K total |
| Bathrooms | Plumbing rough-in, tile, fixtures, vanity | $6K–$15K per bath |
| Electrical | Panel upgrade (if needed), rewiring, fixtures | $3K–$10K |
| HVAC | Replacement vs service call | $4K–$8K |

**Pro tip:** Get at least two contractor bids for any repair estimate over $5,000. Write down every assumption and revisit them before purchase.

## Step 2: The ARV (After Repair Value) — And Why It's the Most Dangerous Number

Your ARV determines everything else in the deal. If you're wrong about ARV, the math collapses no matter how accurate your repair estimate is.

**Three methods for determining ARV:**

1. **Comparable Sales (Comps):** Find 3–5 recently sold properties within a 0.5-mile radius that are similar in size, age, condition (post-renovation), and lot size. Adjust for differences: +$X per extra bedroom, -$Y for smaller lot, etc. This is the gold standard.

2. **Price Per Square Foot:** Take the average price/sqft of your comps, multiply by subject property's sqft. Use cautiously — it smooths over important qualitative differences.

3. **The 70% Rule (Quick Screen):** Maximum purchase price = (ARV × 0.70) – repair costs. This built-in buffer accounts for unexpected issues and profit margin. If a deal doesn't pass the 70% rule, it needs serious scrutiny.

**Red flags on ARV:**
- All comps are from different neighborhoods
- Comps sold in a hot market that's now cooling
- You're using list prices instead of closed sales data
- The comp properties had family members do the renovations (free labor skews results)

## Step 3: The Full Deal Math — Beyond Just Purchase Price + Repairs

A profitable flip needs more than purchase price plus repairs. Here's the complete equation every serious flipper should track:

```
Total Investment = Purchase Price + Closing Costs + Repair Budget
               + Holding Costs (months × monthly carrying costs)
               + Financing Costs (points + interest during hold period)
               + Permit & Inspection Fees
               + Contingency Reserve (10–15% of repairs)
               
Net Profit = ARV – Total Investment – Selling Costs (typically 6–8%)
```

**Example — Austin, TX flip:**

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Purchase Price | $245,000 |
| Closing Costs (buyer) | $4,900 |
| Repair Budget | $52,000 |
| Holding Costs (6 months @ $1,200/mo) | $7,200 |
| Hard Money Interest (6 months @ 12%) | $13,200 |
| Permit/Inspection Fees | $2,500 |
| Contingency Reserve (12.5% of repairs) | $6,500 |
| **Total Investment** | **$331,300** |
| ARV (conservative comp-based) | $425,000 |
| Selling Costs (7%) | ($29,750) |
| **Net Profit** | **$63,950** |
| **ROI** | **19.3%** |

This deal passes with room to breathe. The contingency reserve absorbed potential overages, and the 19% ROI is above the typical minimum threshold of 15% for flips in this market.

## Step 4: The Deal Scoring Matrix (The System That Changes Everything)

Running through all these calculations manually for every deal you evaluate creates decision fatigue and inconsistency. That's why I built a **Deal Scoring Matrix** — a structured evaluation framework that scores each deal across deterministic categories:

- **Financial viability:** ROI, profit margin, cash-on-cash return

## Step 5: Due Diligence — The Checklist That Saves Deals (and Money)

Even a mathematically sound deal can go sideways if you miss critical due diligence items. Here's my 6-phase verification framework:

### Phase 1: Zoning & Entitlements
- Current zoning classification matches intended use?
- Any pending zoning changes in the area?
- Setback requirements and lot coverage limits?

### Phase 2: Environmental
- Flood zone designation (FEMA maps)
- Historical contamination risk (industrial neighbors, former gas stations)
- Soil conditions and drainage patterns

### Phase 3: Structural
- Foundation inspection (non-negotiable)
- Roof age and condition
- Electrical panel capacity and wiring type
- Plumbing material (polybutylene = red flag)

### Phase 4: Financial
- Property tax assessment vs. actual value
- Homestead exemption status (affects transfer taxes)
- HOA fees and restrictions on flips/renovations

### Phase 5: Legal
- Title search for liens, easements, encroachments
- Permits history — were previous renovations permitted?
- Neighbor disputes or boundary disagreements

### Phase 6: Market
- Absorption rate (months of inventory)
- Days on market trend (accelerating or decelerating?)
- New construction competition in the area

**Every single item gets checked.** Not "touched." Checked. If you skip one, you're gambling — not investing.
