# Real Estate Due Diligence Checklist: 6 Phases Every Investor Must Complete

*Last updated: August 2026 | Read time: ~10 minutes*

---

## The $50,000 Mistake Nobody Talks About

A good deal with incomplete due diligence is still a bad deal. Period.

I've seen experienced investors skip inspections to "move fast," ignore title issues because the seller was pushy, and waive environmental checks on commercial properties they'd never walk through in person. The result? $50K–$200K surprises that turn profitable deals into disasters.

Here's the 6-phase due diligence framework I use for every real estate transaction — residential, multifamily, commercial, or development. It takes about 30 days to complete properly (standard inspection period), but it could save you from catastrophic losses.

---

## Phase 1: Zoning & Entitlements

Zoning issues are the silent deal-killers. You can love a property, have perfect financing, and close with cash — if the zoning doesn't allow your intended use, you've bought an empty lot with debt attached to it.

### What to Check
- **Current zoning classification** — Verify with the local planning department, not just the listing description
- **Permitted uses** — Does your intended use (residential, commercial, mixed-use) match what's allowed?
- **Setback requirements** — Can you build what you want within the required distances from property lines?
- **Lot coverage limits** — What percentage of the lot can be developed? Critical for land deals.
- **Parking requirements** — Minimum parking spaces per unit or square foot of commercial space
- **Height restrictions** — Important for multi-story developments
- **Pending zoning changes** — Is there a proposal to rezone your property or neighboring properties that could help or hurt the deal?

### Red Flags
- Property is "grandfathered" under old zoning (non-conforming use) — these can be lost if you make significant changes
- Adjacent properties have different zoning classifications (potential for incompatible uses nearby)
- Local government has proposed infrastructure projects (highways, commercial zones) that could negatively impact the property

### Cost: $0–$500
Most zoning information is publicly available online through municipal websites. A quick call or visit to the planning department confirms everything.

## Phase 2: Environmental Assessment

Environmental issues can create liability that lasts decades — even after you sell the property. The EPA's "innocent landowner defense" exists, but it requires documented due diligence before purchase. Skipping environmental checks is financial negligence.

### What to Check
- **Flood zone designation** (FEMA maps) — Properties in flood zones require expensive insurance and have stricter building requirements
- **Phase I Environmental Site Assessment (ESA)** — For commercial properties or any property with historical industrial use
- **Historical land use** — Was there ever a gas station, auto repair shop, dry cleaner, or manufacturing facility on the site? These activities commonly contaminate soil and groundwater.
- **Underground storage tanks** — Former fuel or chemical storage tanks are still present under thousands of commercial properties
- **Radon levels** — Especially important in certain geological regions (Texas has varying radon potential by county)
- **Soil conditions** — Expansive clay soils (common in Texas) can cause foundation damage worth $20K–$100K to repair

### Red Flags
- Property is in a 100-year floodplain (FEMA Zone AE, AH, or VE)
- Adjacent properties have historical contamination (look at old aerial photos and city directories)
- Soil reports show high clay content or poor drainage
- Nearby industrial facilities with known environmental violations

### Cost: $500–$3,500
Flood zone lookup is free. A Phase I ESA typically costs $2,000–$3,500 for commercial properties. Radon testing runs $150–$300. Foundation/soil assessments run $400–$800.

## Phase 3: Structural Inspection

This is the big one — literally. Structural problems are expensive because they're embedded in the building itself. You can't paint over a cracked foundation.

### What to Check
- **Foundation type and condition** — Slab, crawl space, or basement? Cracks (horizontal cracks are more serious than vertical), settling patterns, moisture intrusion
- **Roof age and condition** — Remaining useful life, visible damage, gutter/downspout functionality
- **Load-bearing walls** — Any previous modifications that removed or altered structural elements without permits
- **Floor system integrity** — Sagging floors, bouncy joists (indicates undersized or damaged framing)
- **Exterior structure** — Brick cracking, stucco separation, wood rot, termite damage

### The Inspector Selection Guide
Not all home inspectors are equal. For properties over $300K or any commercial deal:
1. Hire a licensed structural engineer for the foundation and load-bearing assessment ($400–$800)
2. Use a general home inspector for overall condition ($350–$600)
3. Add specialized inspections as needed (roof, pest/termite, electrical panel, HVAC)

### Red Flags
- Horizontal foundation cracks (indicates lateral pressure — serious structural issue)
- Doors/windows that no longer close properly (foundation movement symptom)
- Water stains on ceilings or walls (active or past leaks)
- Musty smells in crawl spaces or basements (moisture/mold risk)

### Cost: $350–$1,200 depending on property size and inspection scope

## Phase 4: Financial Verification

Even if the property is structurally sound, bad financials can make it a money loser. This phase verifies that all numbers match reality.

### What to Check
- **Property tax assessment** — Compare assessed value to purchase price and recent comparable sales
- **Homestead exemption status** — Affects transfer taxes and future tax burden
- **HOA fees and restrictions** — Monthly/annual costs, special assessments pending, rules that limit your use (rental restrictions, renovation limits)
- **Utility costs history** — Request 12 months of utility bills to verify actual operating expenses
- **Insurance quotes** — Get actual insurance quotes before closing, not estimates
- **Rent documentation** — For income properties, verify all rents are documented in leases and match what the seller reports

### The "Seller Says" Trap
Never accept a seller's word on financials without verification. I've seen:
- Sellers report $2,000/month rent when actual collected rent was $1,650 (vacancy/credit loss hidden)
- Sellers omit $400/month water/sewer costs that the owner pays
- Sellers don't disclose upcoming HOA special assessments of $8K–$15K per unit

### Red Flags
- Property taxes increased dramatically in recent years (assessment spike)
- HOA financial statements show low reserves and pending special assessments
- Utility bills are abnormally high compared to similar properties (possible hidden issues like leaks or inefficient systems)
- Seller refuses to provide rent rolls or operating expense documentation

### Cost: $0–$200 for document requests; insurance quotes are free

## Phase 5: Legal & Title Review

Legal issues can prevent you from using the property as intended, trigger unexpected liabilities, or even result in loss of ownership. This phase is non-negotiable.

### What to Check
- **Title search** — Any liens (tax liens, mechanic's liens, judgment liens), easements, encroachments, or boundary disputes?
- **Permit history** — Were all previous renovations, additions, and structural changes permitted and signed off by the local authority?
- **Survey** — Confirm property boundaries match what the seller claims. Encroachments (neighbor's fence on your property, driveway crossing property lines) are common and expensive to resolve.
- **Easements** — Utility easements, access easements, conservation easements that restrict development
- **Pending litigation** — Is the property involved in any lawsuits? (Neighbor disputes, zoning violations with pending penalties)
- **Zoning compliance** — Does the current use match what's permitted under existing zoning?

### The Permit History Trap
Unpermitted work is one of the most common legal issues in real estate transactions. Problems include:
- Added bathrooms without plumbing permits
- Deck or patio construction without building permits
- Electrical panel upgrades done by unlicensed contractors
- Room additions that exceed setback requirements

If you buy a property with unpermitted work, you inherit the liability. Some municipalities require removal of unpermitted improvements; others force you to retrofit them to current code during any future renovation.

### Red Flags
- Seller is evasive about permit history
- Property has obvious modifications (room additions, pool, detached garage) but no record of permits
- Title search reveals mechanic's liens from contractors who weren't paid
- Survey shows boundary discrepancies with neighbors' properties

### Cost: $500–$1,500 for title search and insurance; survey costs $400–$800

## Phase 6: Market Analysis

The property might be structurally sound, financially verified, legally clear — but if the market is heading south, your investment thesis may not hold. This final phase validates that you're buying into a market with favorable fundamentals.

### What to Check
- **Absorption rate** (months of inventory) — Below 5 months = seller's market (prices rising). Above 7 months = buyer's market (prices softening or declining)
- **Days on market trend** — Are properties selling faster or slower than they did 6–12 months ago? Accelerating sales indicate strengthening demand.
- **New construction activity** — New developments add supply. If you're buying in an area with significant new construction coming online, your resale competition will be intense.
- **Employment trends** — Is the local economy adding jobs or losing them? Major employers leaving town is a negative signal; major employers expanding is positive.
- **Population growth/decline** — Census data shows whether the area is growing or shrinking
- **School district quality** — Even if you're not buying for school districts, families represent the largest buyer pool and school quality directly impacts property values
- **Planned development nearby** — New highways, shopping centers, schools, parks (positive). Landfills, industrial zones, high-voltage power lines (negative)

### The Market Timing Question
Every investor wants to know: "Is now a good time to buy?" The answer depends entirely on your strategy and hold period. A 10-year buy-and-hold investor in a slightly softening market can still achieve excellent returns through cash flow + appreciation recovery. A flipper needs to be more precise about market timing because the hold period is short.

### Red Flags
- Absorption rate has doubled in the past year (market cooling rapidly)
- Major employer announced layoffs or relocation
- New supply pipeline exceeds historical absorption capacity by 30%+
- Property values have declined for 4+ consecutive quarters with no stabilization signs

### Cost: $0–$200 — Most market data is publicly available through MLS, census.gov, BLS, and local planning departments. Paid reports from research firms (CoStar, Reis) are expensive ($5K+) but unnecessary for individual investors.

## The Due Diligence Checklist Summary

| Phase | Key Deliverable | Typical Cost | Red Flag Priority |
|-------|----------------|-------------|-------------------|
| 1. Zoning & Entitlements | Zoning verification letter | $0–$500 | 🔴 Critical — deal stopper if wrong |
| 2. Environmental | FEMA flood zone, Phase I ESA (if applicable) | $500–$3,500 | 🟠 High — liability risk |
| 3. Structural | Inspection report + engineer assessment | $350–$1,200 | 🔴 Critical — repair costs can exceed profit |
| 4. Financial Verification | Tax records, rent rolls, utility bills, insurance quotes | $0–$200 | 🟠 High — affects cash flow projections |
| 5. Legal & Title | Title search, survey, permit history review | $900–$2,300 | 🔴 Critical — ownership and usage rights |
| 6. Market Analysis | Absorption rate, employment trends, supply pipeline | $0–$200 | 🟡 Medium — timing risk |

**Total estimated due diligence cost: $1,750–$7,250**

This is the price of insurance against catastrophic losses. Every single item on this checklist gets verified before I commit capital to a deal. Not "touched." Verified. Documented. If one phase reveals a hard blocker, the deal is dead — no exceptions.
