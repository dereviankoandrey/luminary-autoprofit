# How to Evaluate Multifamily Investment Properties: A Complete Underwriting Guide

*Last updated: August 2026 | Read time: ~10 minutes*

---

## Why Multifamily Investing Is Different (And Harder) Than Single-Family Flips

Most real estate investors start with single-family homes or fix-and-flip deals. They're simpler, cheaper to enter, and easier to understand. But when you move to multifamily — even a modest 4–12 unit building — the underwriting complexity multiplies exponentially.

One bad assumption on a 50-unit property can cost six figures. One optimistic rent estimate on a duplex? Maybe $200. The margin for error shrinks as unit count grows, but so does the opportunity.

Here's how to evaluate multifamily properties with the same deterministic framework I've used across decades of development — from small 4-unit buildings to mixed-use projects valued at over $1 billion.

---

## Step 1: Understand the Two Valuation Methods

Multifamily properties are valued differently than single-family homes. You won't find them priced strictly by comparable sales in most cases. Instead, two methods dominate:

### The Income Approach (Primary Method)
Appraisers and institutional buyers value multifamily based on what it produces, not what similar houses sold for. The key metric is the **capitalization rate** (cap rate):

```
Cap Rate = Net Operating Income (NOI) ÷ Property Value
Property Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate
```

For example: A 12-unit building with $180,000 annual NOI selling at a 6.5% cap rate values at approximately $2.77 million.

### The Comparable Sales Approach (Supporting Method)
Price per unit is sometimes used for quick comparisons, especially in smaller markets:

```
Price Per Unit = Total Sale Price ÷ Number of Units
```

But this method can be misleading — a 4-unit building will almost always have a higher price per unit than a 20-unit building because of economies of scale and management efficiency. Use comps as a sanity check, not the primary valuation tool.

## Step 2: The NOI Deep Dive (Where Real Investors Separate Themselves)

Net Operating Income is everything in multifamily underwriting. Every other calculation flows from it. And most buyers underestimate expenses by 15–30%.

**NOI = Gross Potential Rent – Vacancy & Credit Loss – Operating Expenses**

Let's break down each component with real numbers for a hypothetical 8-unit Austin property:

### Gross Potential Rent (GPR)
Don't use current rents if they're below market. Use **market rent** — what similar units in the area actually lease for:

| Unit | Current Rent | Market Rent | Difference |
|------|-------------|-------------|------------|
| 1 | $1,200 | $1,450 | +$250 |
| 2 | $1,200 | $1,450 | +$250 |
| 3 | $1,350 | $1,550 | +$200 |
| 4 | $1,100 | $1,375 | +$275 |
| 5 | $1,400 | $1,600 | +$200 |
| 6 | $1,250 | $1,475 | +$225 |
| 7 | $1,300 | $1,500 | +$200 |
| 8 | $1,150 | $1,400 | +$250 |
| **Total** | **$10,950/mo** | **$12,800/mo** | **+$1,850/mo** |

This is the #1 value-add opportunity on any multifamily deal: **rent roll compression**. If current rents are 15% below market (as in this example), simply raising rents to market level increases NOI by $22,200 annually without spending a dollar on improvements.

### Vacancy & Credit Loss
Conservative assumption: 5–8% of GPR for larger buildings (8+ units). Smaller buildings may run higher if tenant turnover is less stable.

For our example: $12,800 × 12 months × 6% = **$9,216 annual vacancy loss**

### Operating Expenses (The Hidden Cost Killer)
This is where deals go from profitable to money-losing. Every expense category matters:

| Expense Category | Annual Estimate | Notes |
|-----------------|----------------|-------|
| Property taxes | $35,000 | Verify current assessment + upcoming changes |
| Insurance | $8,400 | Multifamily policies are higher than single-family |
| Property management | $15,360 | 12% of effective gross income (standard) |
| Maintenance & repairs | $9,600 | ~$100/unit/month reserve is a reasonable baseline |
| Utilities (if owner-paid) | $7,200 | Water/sewer often the biggest item |
| Landscaping | $3,600 | Varies significantly by property size/climate |
| Trash removal | $2,400 | Often overlooked in quick analyses |
| Legal & accounting | $3,000 | Annual compliance, lease reviews, tax prep |
| Miscellaneous reserve | $2,400 | Unexpected expenses buffer |
| **Total Operating Expenses** | **$86,568** | |

### Calculate NOI
```
Gross Potential Rent:        $153,600
Less Vacancy (6%):           ($9,216)
Effective Gross Income:      $144,384
Less Operating Expenses:     ($86,568)
Net Operating Income:        $57,816
```

At a 6.5% cap rate, this property would value at approximately **$889,000**. If the asking price is below that, you have equity built into the numbers immediately.

## Step 3: The Debt Service Calculation (Cash Flow Reality Check)

NOI doesn't tell you if the deal cash flows. You need to factor in mortgage payments:

```
Annual Debt Service = Monthly P&I Payment × 12
Monthly Cash Flow = NOI – Annual Debt Service
Cash-on-Cash Return = Annual Cash Flow ÷ Total Cash Invested
```

Using our example with a 75% LTV loan at 7.0% over 30 years:

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Purchase Price | $850,000 |
| Loan (75% LTV) | $637,500 |
| Down Payment + Closing Costs | $212,500 |
| Monthly P&I (~$4,240) | $50,880/year |
| NOI | $57,816 |
| **Annual Cash Flow** | **$6,936** |
| **Monthly Cash Flow** | **$578** |
| **Cash-on-Cash Return** | **3.3%** |

This is a marginal deal at these numbers. The 3.3% cash-on-cash return barely beats a high-yield savings account, and that's before factoring in property appreciation (or depreciation). This would land in the REVIEW category — not automatically NO-GO, but requiring either: lower purchase price, higher rents achieved faster, or reduced acquisition costs.

## Step 4: Value-Add Scenarios (Where Real Wealth Gets Built)

Multifamily's biggest advantage over single-family is that you can actively increase value through operational improvements. Here are the most impactful levers:

### Rent Roll Compression
As shown above, bringing rents to market level added $22,200 in annual NOI on our example — worth approximately **$341,500** at a 6.5% cap rate. That's value created without structural changes.

### Expense Reduction
If property management runs 10% instead of 12%, or if you renegotiate landscaping contracts, every dollar saved in expenses flows directly to NOI and therefore property value.

### Unit Renovations (The "Forced Appreciation" Play)
Cosmetic upgrades per unit typically increase market rent by $75–$200/month depending on the market:

| Upgrade | Cost | Monthly Rent Increase | Annual NOI Impact | Payback Period |
|---------|------|----------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Paint + flooring (Unit 3) | $4,500 | +$100/mo | $1,200 | 4.5 years |
| Kitchen refresh (Unit 7) | $6,000 | +$150/mo | $1,800 | 4.0 years |
| Bathroom update (Unit 1) | $5,500 | +$125/mo | $1,500 | 4.4 years |

At a 6.5% cap rate, each renovation's NOI increase is multiplied by ~15.4x in property value impact. A $4,500 investment that adds $1,200/year of NOI creates approximately **$18,460** in property value — a 309% return on the renovation cost alone (not counting the rent increase).

## Step 5: The Multifamily Due Diligence Checklist

Multifamily due diligence runs deeper than single-family because you're evaluating both a real estate asset and an operating business. Every item matters:

### Physical Inspection
- **Roof:** Age, remaining useful life, replacement cost per sqft
- **HVAC systems:** Central or individual? Service history? Replacement timeline?
- **Foundation:** Cracks, settling, drainage issues (especially in Texas)
- **Plumbing:** Pipe material, water pressure, recent service calls
- **Electrical:** Panel capacity for current unit count + future renovations

### Financial Documentation
- **Past 3 years of rent rolls** — verify occupancy trends and tenant turnover
- **Operating statements** for the same period — identify expense creep
- **Lease agreements** — expiration dates, rent escalation clauses, security deposits
- **Service contracts** — landscaping, trash, security, pest control (can these be renegotiated?)
- **Capital expenditure history** — what's been maintained? What's deferred?

### Market Analysis
- **Absorption rates** for the submarket — new supply coming online?
- **Employment trends** — is the area adding jobs or losing them?
- **School district quality** — affects family tenant pool and long-term value
- **Planned development nearby** — competitors, infrastructure improvements, or negative externalities

### Legal & Compliance
- **Certificate of Occupancy** current and valid for all units?
- **Permit history** for any unpermitted work?
- **Zoning compliance** for unit count and use classification?
- **Environmental reports** if property has historical contamination risk?

## The Bottom Line

Multifamily underwriting is where discipline separates profitable investors from the rest. The math doesn't lie, and it rewards operators who:

1. Use market rents (not current rents) in their pro formas
2. Budget conservatively for expenses — then find ways to reduce them operationally
3. Model multiple scenarios: base case, optimistic case, stress test (what if vacancy hits 10%?)
4. Complete every due diligence item before committing capital
